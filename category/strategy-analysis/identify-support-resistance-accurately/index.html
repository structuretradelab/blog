Support and resistance are the foundation of technical analysis. Every strategy, every indicator, every pattern — they all come back to these levels.

But here's the problem: most beginners draw them wrong. They draw too many. They draw through candle bodies. They force levels that aren't there. And then they wonder why price doesn't react the way they expected.

In this guide, let's walk through how to identify support and resistance levels accurately — like a professional trader.

💡 Key Takeaway: Support and resistance are not exact prices — they're zones. Think in areas, not lines. A level is only valid if price has reacted to it at least 2-3 times.

What Are Support and Resistance?

Support: A price level where buying pressure is strong enough to stop a decline. Think of it as a floor.

Resistance: A price level where selling pressure is strong enough to stop a rally. Think of it as a ceiling.

These levels exist because traders remember past prices. When price fell to 1.1000 and bounced, traders remember that level. When price returns, they buy again — creating support.

📊 Why They Work: Support and resistance work because of human psychology. Traders remember where price bounced or rejected before. Those memories create clusters of buy and sell orders.

Zones, Not Lines

This is the #1 mistake beginners make: they draw support and resistance as exact lines.

Reality: Price doesn't reverse at an exact pip. It reverses in a zone — usually 5-15 pips wide.

How to draw zones:

  • Draw a rectangle (or two horizontal lines) around the area where price reversed
  • The zone should encompass the wicks, not just the closes
  • Use a 5-15 pip range depending on the pair and timeframe
⚠️ Important: If you draw an exact line at 1.1000 and price reverses at 1.0995, your line would have missed it. A zone from 1.0995-1.1005 would have captured it. Always use zones.

How to Draw Accurate Levels

Step 1: Identify the Swing Points

Look for areas where price clearly reversed. These are your swing highs (peaks) and swing lows (troughs).

Step 2: Draw the Zone

Place a horizontal zone that covers the wick area of the reversal. Don't draw through the candle body — use the wicks as your guide.

Step 3: Look for Multiple Touches

A level is only valid if price has touched it at least 2-3 times. One touch is just a random reversal. Two touches suggest a level. Three touches confirm it.

Step 4: Prioritize Higher Timeframes

A level on the daily chart is much stronger than a level on the 15-minute chart. Start with the daily, then move to 4-hour, then 1-hour.

📌 My Process: I start with the weekly chart, then daily, then 4-hour. I draw only the major levels — no more than 3-5 on each timeframe. I use a 10-pip zone for the 4-hour chart and a 20-pip zone for the daily.

What Makes a Level Valid?

Not every price point is a valid support/resistance level. Here's what makes one:

Factor Weak Level Strong Level
Number of touches 1 touch 3+ touches
Timeframe 5-minute Daily/Weekly
Reaction size Small bounce Large reversal
Volume Low volume High volume
Confluence Isolated level Aligns with Fibonacci, trend line, or round number
⚠️ The Bottom Line: A single touch is not a level. A level with 3+ touches on the daily chart is a level you can trust.

Which Timeframes to Use

Support and resistance levels exist on every timeframe. But higher timeframes are always more significant.

Timeframe Level Strength Best For
Weekly Very Strong Major turning points, long-term positions
Daily Strong Swing trading, key levels
4-Hour Moderate Intraday trading, entry timing
1-Hour Weak Entry refinement
15-Minute Very Weak Not recommended for levels

My approach: I identify major levels on the weekly and daily charts. Then I look for entry setups on the 4-hour and 1-hour charts. This alignment gives me the highest probability trades.

Role Reversal: When Support Becomes Resistance

One of the most powerful concepts in support and resistance is role reversal.

When a support level breaks, it often becomes resistance. When a resistance level breaks, it often becomes support.

Example: EUR/USD had support at 1.1000. Price breaks down through 1.1000. Now, when price returns to 1.1000 from below, that level acts as resistance.

Why it happens: Traders who bought at support are now trapped. When price returns, they sell to break even — creating selling pressure.

📌 How to Trade It: After a support breaks, look to sell at the broken support level (now resistance). After a resistance breaks, look to buy at the broken resistance level (now support).

Common Mistakes

1. Drawing too many levels

Your chart should have 3-5 key levels, not 20. More levels = more confusion. Only draw levels that have been tested multiple times.

2. Drawing through candle bodies

Always draw through wicks. The wicks show where price was rejected. The bodies are just the open/close range.

3. Using exact lines instead of zones

Price doesn't reverse at an exact pip. Use zones (5-15 pips) to capture the area.

4. Ignoring higher timeframes

A level on the 1-hour chart means little if the daily chart is trending through it. Always check the higher timeframe first.

5. Forcing levels

If a level hasn't been touched 2-3 times, it's not a level. Don't draw lines just to have lines on your chart.

⚠️ My Rule: I only draw levels that have been tested at least 2-3 times on the daily or 4-hour chart. If a level doesn't meet that criteria, I ignore it.

Simplify Your Chart

Support and resistance are simple concepts, but they're easy to overcomplicate. Draw fewer levels. Use zones, not lines. Focus on higher timeframes. Trade only the best levels.
