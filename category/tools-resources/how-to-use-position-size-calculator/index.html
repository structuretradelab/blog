Input by Input Guide

The calculator asks for five inputs. Here's what each one means:

1. Account Balance (USD)

Your current account balance. Example: 10000 for $10,000. Update this whenever your balance changes significantly.

2. Risk Percentage (%)

The percentage of your account you're willing to lose on this trade. 1% is the standard recommendation. The calculator will show you the dollar amount this represents.

3. Stop Loss (Pips)

The distance from your entry to your stop loss, measured in pips. Example: If you enter at 1.1050 and your stop is at 1.1030, your stop loss is 20 pips.

4. Currency Pair

Select the pair you're trading from the dropdown. The calculator supports 11 pairs including EUR/USD, GBP/USD, USD/JPY, and Gold (XAU/USD).

5. Current Market Price (for certain pairs)

For pairs where USD is not the quote currency, you'll see an additional input asking for the current market price. This ensures accurate pip value calculations.

Why Live Market Prices Matter

Most position size calculators assume you're trading USD-quoted pairs (like EUR/USD). When you trade pairs like USD/JPY or EUR/GBP, the pip value changes based on exchange rates.

Example: The pip value of USD/JPY at 150.00 is different from the pip value at 145.00. If you don't use the live price, your position size will be wrong.

The calculator handles this automatically. You just enter the current rate, and it adjusts the pip value accordingly.

⚠️ Important: Always enter the current market price — not yesterday's price. Rates change constantly. An outdated price means an inaccurate calculation.

Common Mistakes

1. Using an outdated account balance

If your balance has changed since your last trade, update it. The calculator uses the balance you enter — not your broker's live balance.

2. Entering the wrong stop loss distance

Double-check your stop loss in pips. A 20-pip stop and a 200-pip stop produce very different position sizes.

3. Forgetting the live price for non-USD pairs

If you're trading USD/JPY or EUR/GBP and skip the live price input, your pip value will be wrong. The calculator will use a default, which may not match current market conditions.

4. Not rounding down

If the calculator says 0.037 lots, trade 0.03 lots — not 0.04. Rounding up increases your risk above 1%.

5. Ignoring spread

The calculator doesn't account for spread. If your stop is 20 pips and the spread is 1.5 pips, your effective risk is 21.5 pips. Factor this in if precision matters.

⚠️ The Bottom Line: The calculator is a tool, not a substitute for judgment. It tells you the math. You still need to decide whether the trade is worth taking.
