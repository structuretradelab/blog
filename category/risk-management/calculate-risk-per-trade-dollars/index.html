You know the 1% rule. You know you should risk 1% per trade. But when you're about to place a trade, the question becomes: How do I calculate my risk in actual dollars?

In this guide, let's walk through how to calculate your risk per trade in dollars — with simple formulas and real examples.

💡 Key Takeaway: Risk in dollars = Account Balance × Risk %. This is your maximum loss if your stop loss is hit. Calculate it before every trade — not after.

Why Calculate Risk in Dollars?

Knowing your risk in dollars matters because:

  • It removes guesswork: You know exactly how much you're risking before you enter
  • It ensures consistency: Every trade has the same risk, regardless of the pair or stop distance
  • It prevents emotional decisions: If your risk is calculated, you can't "accidentally" risk too much
  • It connects to position size: Your dollar risk determines how many lots you trade
📌 The Rule: Calculate your risk in dollars BEFORE you place the trade. Not after. Not during. Before.

The Formula

The formula for calculating risk in dollars is simple:

Risk in Dollars = Account Balance × Risk %

Example:

  • Account Balance: $10,000
  • Risk %: 1%
  • Risk in Dollars = $10,000 × 0.01 = $100

That $100 is your maximum loss if your stop loss is hit. Not $50, not $200 — exactly $100.

⚠️ Important: Your risk in dollars is not your position size. It's the amount you're willing to lose. Your position size is calculated based on this risk amount and your stop loss distance.

How Risk in Dollars Connects to Position Size

Once you know your risk in dollars, you can calculate your position size:

Position Size = Risk in Dollars ÷ (Stop Loss in Pips × Pip Value)

Complete example:

  • Account: $5,000
  • Risk: 1% = $50
  • Stop loss: 25 pips
  • Pair: EUR/USD (pip value $10 per standard lot, $0.10 per micro lot)
  • Position size = $50 ÷ (25 × $0.10) = $50 ÷ $2.50 = 20 micro lots (0.20 lots)

Another example (USD/JPY):

  • Account: $10,000
  • Risk: 1% = $100
  • Stop loss: 30 pips
  • Pair: USD/JPY at 150.00 (pip value $6.67 per standard lot)
  • Position size = $100 ÷ (30 × $6.67) = $100 ÷ $200 = 0.50 lots

Common Mistakes

1. Calculating risk AFTER entering the trade

Some traders enter first, then check their risk. This is backwards. Calculate before you enter.

2. Using the wrong account balance

If your account balance changes (after wins/losses), recalculate your risk. Your risk should be based on your current balance, not last month's balance.

3. Forgetting that risk changes with stop distance

Your risk in dollars stays the same (1%), but your position size changes based on stop distance. Wider stops = smaller position. Tighter stops = larger position.

4. Not accounting for spread

Your effective risk is slightly higher than your calculated risk because of the spread. If your stop is 20 pips and the spread is 1 pip, your effective risk is 21 pips. Adjust your calculation if precision matters.

⚠️ The Bottom Line: Calculating risk in dollars is simple, but it's easy to forget. Make it a habit. Calculate before every trade. No exceptions.
